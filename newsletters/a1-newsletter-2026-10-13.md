# A1 AI Skills Newsletter

Hello,

Employees often begin using AI before a business has written any rules. Today’s lesson helps a small team create a simple starting guide that answers the questions people face during ordinary work.

## Today’s lesson: Draft a one-page AI use guide

A useful starter guide answers five questions:

1. **Which tools and accounts are approved?** Name the exact services and require business-managed accounts where appropriate.
2. **What information stays out?** Identify customer, employee, payment, health, identity, legal, security, credential, and confidential business data that may not be entered without specific approval.
3. **Which tasks are allowed?** Start with low-risk drafting, brainstorming, formatting, or summarizing approved material.
4. **What must a person review?** Require review before anything reaches a customer, changes a record, publishes a claim, affects money, or influences a consequential decision.
5. **Who handles questions and mistakes?** Name the policy owner and explain how to report accidental data entry, incorrect output, or unexpected tool behavior promptly.

The guide should reflect the business’s actual tools, contracts, legal duties, and data. A generic template is only a starting point.

## Try this in 30 minutes

1. List every AI tool currently used for business work, including browser extensions and AI features inside other software.
2. Mark each tool `Approved`, `Under review`, or `Not approved`.
3. Write three examples of permitted low-risk tasks.
4. Write the categories of information that must not be entered.
5. Name the person who reviews external or high-impact output.
6. Write one sentence explaining how to report a mistake.
7. Add an effective date and the next review date.
8. Test the guide with three realistic employee questions and revise unclear rules.

Do not ask an AI tool to review or summarize confidential policy, contract, customer, or employee information unless that tool and use are approved for the data involved.

## Copy this prompt

```text
Act as a cautious policy-drafting assistant. Create a plain-language, one-page starter
guide from the owner-approved decisions below. Include: purpose, approved tools and
accounts, permitted tasks, prohibited data and uses, required human review, incident
reporting, policy owner, effective date, and review date.

Do not invent legal requirements, vendor protections, certifications, permissions, or
business decisions. Replace missing decisions with [OWNER TO DECIDE]. Add three short
workplace scenarios that employees can use to test whether the rules are clear. State
that qualified legal, privacy, HR, security, and industry review may be required.

Owner-approved decisions:
[Insert decisions without restricted or confidential information]
```

## Human review checklist

- Tools are named specifically instead of saying “approved AI.”
- Business work uses the correct managed account.
- Prohibited data and prohibited actions are clear.
- Permitted tasks include realistic examples.
- Customer-facing, financial, legal, HR, safety, and other high-impact work has a named reviewer.
- Staff know how and when to report a mistake.
- The policy owner, effective date, and review date are listed.
- The guide is matched to contracts, laws, industry rules, and professional advice where needed.

## A beginner service you could offer

Offer an **AI Use Guide Workshop** for very small businesses. Interview the owner, inventory tools, organize their decisions, create a one-page draft, and run three staff scenarios. Do not sell the result as legal compliance, cybersecurity certification, privacy approval, or an employment policy unless qualified professionals have reviewed it.

## Skill ladder

- **Easy:** Inventory tools and turn owner decisions into a one-page guide.
- **Medium:** Add role-specific examples, staff training, incident reporting, and scheduled reviews.
- **Hard:** Build an AI governance program with vendor assessment, access controls, data classification, testing, monitoring, audit records, and legal and security oversight.

## The takeaway

A short policy people can use is better than vague permission or a long document nobody reads. Name the tools, protect the data, keep people responsible, and make mistakes easy to report.
